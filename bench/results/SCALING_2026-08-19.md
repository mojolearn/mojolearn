# The scaling curve. The bet loses, and it loses for a specific reason.

Apple M4. Ours against scikit-learn on its DEFAULT algorithm, same data, same
sizes. **scikit-learn gets `algorithm='auto'`, not `'brute'`**, because
forcing it to brute force would flatter us by making it run something no real
user would run.

## k-NN, 2,000 queries, 32 features

    n index     ours ms   sklearn ms   ratio
     20,000       15.78        30.62   1.94x
     50,000       39.28        64.39   1.64x
    100,000       79.31       116.82   1.47x
    200,000      156.81       227.08   1.45x
    400,000      305.78       501.09   1.64x

Both linear. Our advantage is FLAT at roughly 1.5x and does not grow. It does
not blow up to 15x at 200k.

## DBSCAN, 8 features, eps 0.30

    n points    ours ms   sklearn ms    ratio
      4,000       19.03        13.30   0.70x
     16,000      274.64        59.93   0.22x
     50,000     2666.20       206.62   0.077x
    100,000    10523.76       499.56   0.047x
    200,000    45937.13      1237.99   0.027x

**We get 37x SLOWER by 200,000 points, and the gap widens with every step.**

## Why, and this is the finding

The bet was "quadratic work on a GPU beats linear work on a CPU once the data
is big enough." At 200k points that is 40 billion pairs, and the reasoning
was that scikit-learn stops being usable there.

**scikit-learn does not do 40 billion pairs.** Its default builds a
kd-tree or ball tree and does O(n log n) neighborhood queries. Our curve is
n^2 (50x the points cost 2,400x the time, which is exactly quadratic). Their
curve is near-linear (50x the points cost 93x the time).

No amount of GPU throughput wins that. A quadratic algorithm on fast hardware
loses to a log-linear algorithm on slower hardware, and it loses by more at
every size. That is the whole result.

k-NN holds its 1.5x because at 32 features scikit-learn's `auto` largely
falls back to brute force too, so both sides are doing the same asymptotic
work and the comparison is hardware against hardware. That is the regime
where our architecture wins, and it is a narrow one.

## What this means, said plainly

**The library as it stands wins on dense linear algebra and loses on spatial
queries at scale.** OLS at 8.33x and PCA are matmul-shaped and real. k-NN is
a modest constant-factor win. DBSCAN past about 10,000 points is not
competitive and cannot be made competitive by tuning kernels.

The fix is not a faster brute force. It is the INDEX. cuVS ships
`neighbors/ball_cover` and the IVF family precisely for this, and
`neighbors/UNPORTED.tsv` records them as "out of scope for now, build brute
force first, measure where it stops winning."

**This is that measurement, and it stopped winning at about 10,000 points.**
That line was written as a formality. It turns out to have been the most
important sentence in the file.

## Caveats on our own number

Our DBSCAN computes distances TWICE per fit: the row batching keeps memory
bounded by recomputing rather than by porting cuML's `MergeLabels`. Removing
that would take roughly a factor of two off our column and change no
conclusion, because the gap is 37x and growing.

Both curves are single-run medians of 3 at each size, alternated at process
level, on a machine that drifts. The DBSCAN trend is far outside any drift.
